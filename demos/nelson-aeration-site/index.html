Why aerate

Your lawn might be holding its breath.

Feet, mowers and rain press the soil tighter every year. Roots run out of room, water runs off instead of soaking in, and feeding the lawn does less than it should. Here's how to tell.

  • Water puddles or runs off

    After rain or sprinklers, water sits on top or runs down the driveway instead of soaking in.

  • Thin, patchy grass

    Bare or weak spots, especially along paths, play areas and where the dog runs.

  • Hard, packed soil

    A screwdriver is hard to push into the lawn after a good watering. That's compaction.

  • Tired despite the care

    You water and feed it, but it still looks dull and never quite fills in.

How it works

Small plugs out. Air, water and roots in.

Core aeration uses a machine that pulls finger-sized plugs of soil out of the lawn and leaves them on top. The holes give the roots room to breathe, drink and spread.

  1. Book a visit

    Pick a time that suits you this season. One button, a short form.

  2. We pull the plugs

    A core aerator works across the lawn in passes, lifting small plugs of soil and leaving them on the surface.

  3. Leave the plugs, water the lawn

    The plugs break down by themselves with rain and mowing, feeding the soil as they go.

  4. Watch it fill in

    With room to breathe, the grass roots deeper and thicker. It's also the best moment to overseed.

When to book

Aerate while your grass is growing.

Grass heals and fills the holes fastest in its own growing season. Skip times when the ground is frozen, waterlogged or bone-dry in a drought.

Cool-season grasses

Early fall, or spring

Kentucky bluegrass, fescues and ryegrass, the usual lawns in cooler, northern climates. Fall is the favourite: warm soil, cool air, and less weed competition.

Warm-season grasses

Late spring to early summer

Bermuda, zoysia and St. Augustine, common in warmer, southern climates. Aerate once the lawn has fully greened up and is growing strongly.

Not sure which grass you have? Mention it when you book and we'll help you pick the right time.

On the day

A little prep goes a long way.

Five minutes before the visit and a little care afterwards make the difference between holes and a thicker lawn.

Before we arrive

  • Mow a day or two before, to your normal height.
  • Water the day before if it's been dry, so the tines go in deep.
  • Mark sprinkler heads, shallow pipes and invisible dog-fence lines with flags.
  • Clear hoses, toys and furniture, and keep pets inside.

After the visit

  • Leave the plugs. They crumble back into the lawn with rain and mowing.
  • Water well over the following days if there's no rain.
  • Overseed or feed now if you planned to; seed and fertiliser reach the soil through the holes.
  • Mow as normal once the plugs have dried out.

Questions

Good to know before you book.

How often should a lawn be aerated?

Most lawns do well with aeration once a year. Heavy clay soil, or a lawn that gets a lot of foot traffic, can benefit from it more often.

Will the plugs make a mess?

For a little while the lawn looks dotted with small soil plugs. Leave them where they are: they break down on their own with rain and mowing, and return nutrients to the soil.

Is aeration the same as dethatching?

No. Dethatching rakes out the layer of dead stems on top of the soil. Core aeration pulls plugs out of the soil itself to relieve compaction. Aeration also helps a thin thatch layer break down.

Can I overseed afterwards?

Yes, and it's the best time to. Seed that falls into the holes has soil contact, moisture and protection, so it takes far better than seed scattered on hard ground.

What about my sprinklers and buried lines?

Mark sprinkler heads, shallow irrigation lines and invisible pet-fence wires with small flags before the visit, so the machine can steer around them.

How much does it cost?

It depends mainly on the size of your lawn. You'll get a price before anything is confirmed.
